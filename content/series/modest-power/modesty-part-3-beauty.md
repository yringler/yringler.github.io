## Modesty Isn't About Power. It's About Beauty.

A while back I argued that modesty was about power — a woman's beauty is a kind of power, and dressing modestly was the responsible handling of it. Contain the power, don't wield it carelessly. It felt right. It was built on a mistake I couldn't see until I tried to make it measurable.

The mistake: if modesty exists to *limit* a woman's power, it should make her less striking, less noticed. It doesn't. Set the provocative stuff aside — that's its own category and everyone knows it. Compare two women both well inside the range of the ordinary: one in a sleeveless top and shorts, one in something modest but genuinely beautiful, cut well and chosen with care. The modest one has *more* presence, not less. If the point was to turn the power down, modest dress fails, because it turns it up.

So the old framing eats itself. And once I saw why, I realized power was never the right word. The thing I was circling was beauty — and beauty, it turns out, isn't about men at all.

### Beauty isn't downstream of male attention

The feminist version of the objection goes: modest dress just makes a woman more pleasant for men to look at; "normal" dress sets her free from performing for them. This assumes beauty is fundamentally *for* men — a lure, a bit of bait, and the only question is whether you're offering it on their terms or your own.

My grandmother had her hair done and her nails done at ninety-six. There was no mate to attract, no rival to outcompete, no male gaze operating in any register a psychologist could measure. The drive was intact anyway. Whatever that is, it is not downstream of men.

It is also not idiosyncratic. The drive to adorn is one of the closest things we have to a human universal. The philosopher Stephen Davies, surveying the archaeology and anthropology of it, defines decoration as making special by aesthetic enhancement, and argues that self-decoration is one of the few social behaviors close to being genuinely universal — a fundamental mode of communication that runs through burial goods, body paint, jewelry, and hair across every culture we've looked at.[^davies] It shows up in contexts with no mating function in view at all. It is far older and far wider than "attracting a man."

But there is a case where dress connects to men — immodest dress. Studies of how women actually choose clothing find that women dress differently for all-female groups than for mixed ones — and specifically, they choose less revealing outfits when the audience is only women, in both social and professional settings.[^krems] The revealing register is the one that tracks the presence of men; researchers found the presence of a romantic interest was among the strongest predictors of choosing more attractive clothing.[^olson] But the women didn't stop dressing carefully for each other — they dressed *differently*, not less. Something is still operating in the all-female room, and it isn't mate attraction.

There are two different things going on, and only one of them is about men. *Exposure* is the register that tracks male attention — revealing dress goes up when a crush is in the room. *Composition* — adornment, taste, how a woman assembles herself — is the older, deeper drive that runs in the all-female group, in front of the mirror alone, everywhere. The feminist objection works by fusing these two into a single thing called "beauty for men." Separate them and the objection falls apart, because the register that isn't about men is the one modest dress is built around.

Someone will answer that exposure can be aesthetic rather than sexual if that's how the woman means it — the same garment, innocent when worn with innocent intent. But signals don't take their meaning from the sender. They take it from the population distribution of who sends them and why. That is what makes a signal a signal rather than a private thought. If revealing dress reliably tracks mate-seeking across a population, then that is what it says, in the only sense of saying that operates in a public visual environment. A woman cannot unilaterally reassign it any more than she can decide that for her, personally, a raised middle finger means good morning. The meaning was set by everyone else's usage. She is borrowing it whether she wants to or not.

And the behavior itself is the confession. Women drop the revealing register in the all-female room. They already know what the garment says, and they switch it deliberately by audience. You cannot then claim the garment is semantically neutral and colored only by intent. Nor does granting the intent help, since meaning is received, not transmitted: the man looking at her cannot read her intentions off her clothing. He reads the code. So the innocent exposure is received as the mating register regardless, and her intention buys her nothing in the only place it would have to cash out.

None of which makes the mating register bad. Between husband and wife it is presumably exactly right. It is a register with a proper domain — and broadcasting it to the street doesn't neutralize it. It detunes it, and takes her older register down with it.

### What exposure trades away

A garment can say something. Its cut, its color, the choice of it — that is an authored signal, and it is the only channel through which taste can speak at all. A body says something too: proportion, symmetry, youth. That signal is given, not made. She did not compose it and cannot revise it.

Exposure does not add a channel. It subtracts one. Skin has no design; there is nothing on a bare arm to compose. So when the garment stops doing work, the authored signal doesn't get replaced by some new signal of the flesh — the flesh was always there, always saying its piece. What changes is that nothing is speaking over it.

And when a single channel carries all the differentiation, the resolution on that channel climbs. Ranking demands a discriminator. Give the eye only one, and it will read that one to the micron.

I once read an account of a woman in a shop, watching a strikingly beautiful stranger try on some ordinary revealing piece, while the saleswoman kept up a running commentary on every small imperfection of the woman's body. It isn't cruelty — it's the logical terminus of the system. The saleswoman was not reading some new signal that exposure had created. She was reading the only signal left once the garment had stopped saying anything.

There is a third signal, and it is the one people think they are buying. In a modest society, a bared arm is loud — it carries a charge precisely because it is a departure, marked against a norm. That is real. Anyone who has felt it is not imagining it. But it is a signal of transgression, not of beauty, and it is the one thing here that cannot survive being spent. The more it is spent, the less it buys. Bare arms everywhere means bare arms say nothing, the way a word repeated a hundred times stops sounding like a word. No individual woman can opt back into it, because the extinction happened to the category, not to any viewer. Novelty is the wasting asset. Habituation is what wastes it.

So exposure trades an authored signal for a transgressive one, and the transgressive one habituates to zero. What is left is anatomy, and a very fine ruler.

### Two layers, and which one this is about

Before going further, a scope note. Tzniut is really two overlapping norms, and I'm only making a claim about one of them.

There's a baseline of covering that holds more or less regardless of audience — which is why standards don't collapse to zero when a woman is alone. And layered on top there's a large, explicitly context-sensitive register that scales with who's present. Halacha says so itself. Take kol isha: a woman's voice is ervah in the relevant mixed setting and carries no problem at all when she sings for women — even the most careful communities hold this without blinking. The voice didn't change; ervah is a relational category, defined over the pairing of person and audience, not a property in the throat. Separate swimming is the same shape: at a women-only beach what a frum woman wears drops well below the street standard, and that isn't a smuggled leniency — the covering genuinely relaxes because the relevant observer is absent.

The argument in this essay is about that relational register — the part halacha *already* treats as observer-sensitive. I'm not deriving the person-indexed baseline from habituation; that stands on other ground. I'm saying the observer-relative layer has a rational structure, and the structure is about beauty's unit of measure, not about managing men. The studies above are describing the same relational layer from the outside — women modulating dress by audience, dropping the exposure register in the all-female room. Halacha formalizes a contour that the empirical work stumbles onto independently.

With that fenced off, back to the channels.

### Body beauty versus person beauty

The authored channel is recoverable, even where the transgressive one isn't, because it was only ever closed by what the clothing stopped doing. Modest-but-beautiful dress puts the garment back to work: composition — taste, choice, the way a person assembles herself. What's being read is *authored*. It's an expression of the person rather than a readout of her anatomy.

To be beautiful in "normal" clothing, you need a beautiful body.
To be beautiful in modest clothing, you can be a beautiful person.

The sharp reader will object that the composed look still sits on a body and still gets judged partly on it — nobody made the flesh disappear. True, and it doesn't hurt the claim, because the claim was never that the body vanishes. It's that the *margin* moves: the axis on which one woman is actually distinguished from another. Under exposure, the margin is all flesh. Under modest adornment, the margin is composition. The body is still there; it just stops being the thing you're ranked on. And composition, unlike bone structure, is something a person can actually do — which is why modest dress levels the field rather than merely re-covering it.

That answers the ranking. It doesn't answer the deeper injury.

### The audience she loses

Her composition stops being receivable in the exposure regime — and it stops twice, by two different routes.

The first route is drowning. Even a viewer who wants to read her composition can't get to it, because in a flooded environment the body is the loudest thing in the frame — it is the only thing speaking. So whatever she assembled on top of it registers as noise around the actual message.

The second route is blindness, and it runs through the decent man. To avoid sexualizing everyone, he has to not notice. And the not-noticing can't be surgical — he can't switch off the body and leave the beauty running, because in this environment the body is the only thing speaking. So the discipline he's forced into is a blanket one. He goes blind to the whole channel, including the part of it she authored. He is not the subject here; he is the evidence. His blindness is the second confirmation that her audience is gone. In an immodest society the available responses narrow to two — consume the body, or see nothing — and neither one is a position from which her beauty is perceived *as hers*.

Modesty restores the third position. When the unit is composition rather than flesh, a man can appreciate a woman's beauty without the appreciation being sexual — not because he is exercising heroic restraint, but because what he is looking at is not her body. He is looking at what she made. Everything blurs at the edges and attraction doesn't evaporate; the honest claim is about what the appreciation is *anchored* in. Under exposure it is anchored in her anatomy, because nothing else is left to anchor it. Under composition it is anchored in what she authored, with the body present but not the unit. That is a difference in kind, even where the edges blur.

So the modest woman doesn't need him to go blind. She gets to be seen, and seen as a person — which is what she wanted in the first place. She keeps the drive either way; that was never in question. My grandmother's drive, the universal one. What exposure takes from her is not the wanting. It is the channel. She is left with an expressive faculty and no possible audience for it — while modest dress gives her the audience back, which is the precondition for the thing she has been doing since she was young enough to want her hair done, and will still be doing at ninety-six.

### What follows

Exposure subtracts the authored signal and cashes in a transgressive one; habituation spends the transgressive signal to nothing; what remains is the given signal, read at a resolution no one can survive. Her composition becomes unreadable and her decent audience blinds itself. One mechanism, two injuries: exposure ranks her by something she didn't choose, and it deletes the audience for the thing she did. Under exposure, beauty is aristocratic — distributed by birth, closed to effort. Under modest adornment it is closer to democratic — reachable through taste, care, and composition — and, just as important, it is *legible*, because there is once again someone positioned to read it.

Which is what finally disposes of "eye candy for men." The charge doesn't just miss — it inverts. Exposure is the eye-candy regime, because it makes the body the unit and the body is the thing that gets consumed. It is the position that guarantees the body is the only thing there is to look at, and then drives every decent man into a blindness that means she is not really looked at at all. Modest dress is the regime where beauty is of the person, and where a person can be seen. The feminist accusation lands squarely on the position she is defending.

### Why this fits Chassidus, and why that matters

There's a deeper account of all this that I hold and don't disown. The verse is *kol kevudah bas melech penimah* — all the glory of the king's daughter is within.[^verse] The Chassidic explanation of this turns on the word *penimah*, "within," being the same root as *pnimiyus*, inwardness.[^crispe] The point was never a woman's effect on observers. Glory is inward because inwardness is its native register: the pnimiyus is what's real and the outer is its garment. Concealment isn't a strategic dialing-down of outward reach. It's where kavod lives.

Look at what the beauty argument arrived at on its own terms. Exposure makes the *body* the unit; modest adornment makes the *person* the unit. That is the guf-and-pnimiyus distinction — outer and inner, garment and what wears it — reached by habituation and anthropology instead of by Chassidus. And the role modest dress plays is precisely the role of a levush. In Tanya the soul's garments — thought, speech, action — are not a covering that diminishes; they are how the inner faculties get expressed at all.[^tanya] The Rebbe Rayatz puts it plainly: through the garment of the body, the soul reveals itself in its true essence.[^rayatz] That's the move — not concealment as negation, but the garment that lets the inner thing be expressed in a form that holds, rather than dumped as raw stimulus and consumed. Even Chabad.org's plainest primer on the subject lands here: modesty isn't a denial of self or a forcing into hiding, but a dignified private space in which the inner self can shine through rather than get drowned out by external charisma.[^primer]

That convergence is the point, and it's stronger than "here's a spiritual version for those who want one." When an argument built only on habituation and the anthropology of adornment lands on *the self is real, the body is its garment, and the garment expresses rather than hides* — and that is the Chassidic structure exactly — the agreement is the tell that the frame is right. The friction I felt with the old power argument was the frame being wrong; it kept the observer at the center, which is why it always read as an answer *to* the feminist rather than a description of something true. This one doesn't need her in the room.

And that's the whole use of it. The Chassidic answer is complete, but it's complete for someone already standing inside it — it asks you to grant, going in, that the inner is what's real and the outer a garment. To a person actually struggling, an answer that only works once you've bought the system can land as no answer at all. The beauty argument asks for none of that. It asks you to take a woman's beauty seriously as her own — which the anthropology backs, and which any ninety-six-year-old getting her hair done will confirm — and to be honest about how habituation works, which anyone can check against their own life. It reaches the same door from premises a skeptic already holds. Not a truer answer. An independently accessible one — and for an emunah that's the thing doing the struggling, the road that doesn't lean on emunah to stand up is exactly the one that can bear weight.
