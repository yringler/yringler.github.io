Someone cuts you off, takes credit for your work, says something cruel. The standard Jewish advice is *dan lekaf zechus*: judge them favorably. Maybe he was rushing to the hospital. Maybe she didn't realize how it sounded.

Sometimes that works. Sometimes the story you're supposed to tell yourself is a stretch, you know it's a stretch, and the mind doesn't give up a judgment because you handed it a story it doesn't believe.

For those cases there's another route, one that asks for less invention.

## Same verdict, both directions

Psychologists call it the fundamental attribution error. When someone else behaves badly, we blame their character. When we behave badly, we blame the circumstances: I was tired, I was provoked, I didn't mean it like that.

The popular version of *dan lekaf zechus* fixes the imbalance by lending the other person circumstances. You can also fix it from the other side, by holding yourself to the same standard you're holding them to.

This person acted badly and struggles with middos. So do I. That's what people are like.

That framing makes no claim about what happened, so it can't be wrong. It doesn't require you to believe the driver was on his way to the hospital. It only requires you to remember that you've also been the driver.

## The sources

This approach has its own grounding in the tradition, alongside the more familiar one.

*Al tadin es chavercha ad shetagia limkomo* (Avos 2:4). Don't judge your fellow until you've reached his place. You never fully reach it, so in practice the verdict is never yours to give.

The Alter Rebbe makes almost exactly this move in Tanya, chapter 30. He doesn't argue that the other person is innocent. He argues that you can't measure his struggle, which may be far harder than yours, and then he turns you to your own failings, which, measured against your own situation, may be worse. The point isn't to acquit him. It's to take away your seat on the bench.

The Rambam, explaining *hevei dan es kol ha'adam lekaf zechus* (Avos 1:6), limits judging favorably to people of good standing and to cases that are truly ambiguous. A known bad actor doesn't get an invented alibi. Halacha has room for realism here.

## Letting go isn't trusting

*Kabdeihu v'chashdeihu*: respect him and suspect him. You can drop the resentment and still lock the door.

## Moving on

The goal of *dan lekaf zechus* was never accurate fact-finding. It was to stop carrying other people's failures around as grievances. If the charitable story gets you there, use it. If it doesn't, there's another way to put it down.
