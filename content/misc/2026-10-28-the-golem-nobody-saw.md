A friend sent me a podcast clip claiming the Golem of Prague story is false. I went and found the source. It's Rabbi Aubrey Hersh on *History for the Curious*, episode 55, the second of two episodes on the Maharal. The argument is good. It also doesn't prove what most people will assume it proves, and the gap between those two things turned out to be more interesting than the golem.

## The case against

Hersh is careful about his starting point. He doesn't say golems are impossible. Kabbalistic sources take golem-making seriously. The Chacham Tzvi records that his ancestor, Rabbi Eliyahu Ba'al Shem of Chelm, made one, and there's a tradition that the Vilna Gaon tried and was stopped. The question Hersh asks is narrower: did the Maharal make one in Prague?

The story as we know it comes almost entirely from *Nifla'os Maharal*, published in 1909 by Rabbi Yudel Rosenberg. Rosenberg said he was printing a manuscript he found in the Royal Library of Metz, written by the Maharal's son-in-law, who had helped build the golem. The book sets the creation in 1572, under Rudolf II, to fight blood libels pushed by a Cardinal Sylvester.

None of it holds up. Rudolf wasn't emperor in 1572. The Maharal wasn't the rav of Prague in 1572 and wasn't even living there yet. There was no Cardinal Sylvester in Prague in any century. There was no Royal Library of Metz.

The other evidence is just as thin. No written source ties a golem to the Maharal until around 1840, roughly 230 years after his death. Gershom Scholem argued that the Chelm legend migrated to the more famous rabbi, a move helped along by the Maharal's well-known private audience with Rudolf, an emperor obsessed with alchemy and the occult. Even the Altneushul's custom of saying *Mizmor Shir L'yom HaShabbos* twice on Friday night, usually explained as the Maharal stopping a rampaging golem before Shabbos, has a more plausible explanation. Kabbalas Shabbos was new, imported from Tzfas. Prague welcomed Shabbos with Lecha Dodi and musical instruments, then repeated the psalm to mark the moment Shabbos began in halacha and the instruments had to stop.

## Rosenberg wasn't lying

The tempting conclusion is that Rosenberg was a forger. Hersh, following Shnayer Leiman, doesn't think so, and I agree.

Late in Rosenberg's life, a volume was published listing his works in three categories: halacha, Kabbalah and drush, and storybooks. *Nifla'os Maharal* is in the third. He wrote these tales so Jewish readers would have something to read besides secular fiction.

The "found manuscript" opening is one of the oldest moves in European fiction. Cervantes claimed *Don Quixote* was translated from an Arabic chronicle he bought as scrap paper. Walpole passed off *The Castle of Otranto* as a sixteenth-century Italian text. Hawthorne found Hester Prynne's story in a custom house attic. Eco stacked three fake editors on top of *The Name of the Rose*. Readers who know the convention read the frame as part of the story.

Rosenberg's frame borrowed something else too: the form of a Jewish source. A named talmid, a manuscript, a chain of transmission. Those are the things a Jewish reader is trained to trust. A literary wink got read as a citation, and once the books circulated without their author's own label, nothing marked them as fiction.

## The argument from silence

The natural next step is the argument from silence, which my friend attributed to the podcast, though Hersh doesn't actually make it. Prague in the late 1500s is unusually well documented. There are tax registers, gravestones, and the chronicles of David Gans, the Maharal's own student, who recorded the audience with Rudolf. A creature that saved the community from blood libels would be a strange thing for everyone to leave out.

That argument only works if a golem would leave traces. Suppose it didn't. Suppose it was made in secret, looked like an ordinary man, and did nothing dramatic. Then the silence is exactly what you'd expect. *Nifla'os Maharal* is the story of the one man who talked. If it's fiction, then nobody talked, and the record would look exactly as it does.

That's convenient, and the convenience cuts both ways. You can't establish a historical fact because the lack of evidence happens to fit. You also can't dismiss an explanation because it's convenient. "How convenient" is a raised eyebrow, not a refutation. If the secret version is wrong, it has to be wrong for a better reason.

## What's left

There is a better reason, and it isn't about convenience. It's about cost. Every source connecting the Maharal to a golem is the legend, and the legend consists of the very parts the secret version has to discard: the blood libels, the rampage, the attic, the psalm. Strip those away as fiction and nothing is left holding up the core claim. You'd be keeping one assertion from a source you've otherwise rejected, and it's the one assertion that can't be checked.

That makes the secret golem unfalsifiable, not supported. It fits the evidence, but so does any secret event in anyone's life.

So there are two pictures. In one, a legend about a rabbi in Chelm drifted to a more famous rabbi who had met an occultist emperor, and a storyteller wrote it up in 1909 with the period's standard fictional frame. In the other, the event happened, left no trace, and the only story about it is fiction. The first explains everything without extra assumptions. The second needs extra assumptions to explain why there's nothing to see.

History doesn't prove negatives; it ranks explanations. The first one ranks higher.

## Whispers

Ranking higher doesn't decide what actually happened, and Hersh knows it. He ends by saying he won't tell listeners what to believe and that he still wouldn't go up into the attic of the Altneushul. Asked about the story of two Nazis climbing the stairs, he says he once spoke with someone who spoke to the synagogue's gabbai, and that he'll only tell the rest in Prague.

That reticence makes sense for a historian. A third-hand account can't be presented on a recording without either flagging it as hearsay, which kills it, or presenting it straight, which undercuts the method he just used on Rosenberg. Kept for the tour, it stays a story told in the place it's about, without claiming to be evidence.

What remains is a whisper of a possibility, and that's the most we could ever have. If the Maharal made a golem and wanted no one to know, then our uncertainty is the plan working. Practical Kabbalah was always meant to be concealed. Prague itself had a long tension between those who hid what they knew and those who revealed it. A perfect silence would be that tradition doing its job, and the cardinal, the library, and the monster in the attic would be what people invented to fill the space.

If it happened, the secret might be the greater feat. According to the sources, making a golem takes knowledge and holiness only a handful of people ever had. Keeping a secret takes discipline, which turns out to be rarer. Small groups manage it occasionally. They almost never manage it for four hundred years.

The same silence fits just as well with nothing having happened. "He hid it perfectly" and "there was nothing to hide" look identical from here, and they always will. The attic door stays closed.
